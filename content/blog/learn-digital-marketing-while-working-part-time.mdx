If you're asking whether you can do a digital marketing course part time while holding down a full-time job, the answer is yes, and you don't need to quit anything to start. Our evening batches run Monday, Wednesday, and Friday from 7 to 9 PM, built for people who clock out at 6 PM and still want to build a real skill. Add recordings with 12-month replay for the days work runs late, and most of our working students manage the whole thing on 10 to 12 hours a week.

I built the schedule this way on purpose. I run a digital marketing institute in Sonipat, and over the years I've watched far more working professionals walk through our doors than college students with free afternoons. Teachers, sales executives, government employees, people running their own small shop. Almost none of them had the luxury of quitting first and figuring out marketing later. So the course had to bend around a job, not the other way around.

## Why This Actually Works Better Than It Sounds

Here's something I tell every working student in their first week: keeping your job while you learn isn't a compromise. It's an advantage. You have a live workplace to practice on every single day, something a fresh graduate simply doesn't have. When you learn a Meta Ads concept on Wednesday night, you can walk into your office on Thursday and ask if you can help run the company's Instagram page. That's not theory anymore. That's a campaign with your name on it.

This matches what's happening more broadly too. LinkedIn's [2025 Workplace Learning Report](https://learning.linkedin.com/resources/workplace-learning-report) found that professionals who keep upskilling while employed are 40% more likely to advance in their careers than those who wait. Learning on the side isn't a fallback plan. It's become the standard path.

## The Realistic Weekly Schedule

Most working students ask me the same question in week one: how do I actually fit this in without burning out? Here's the breakdown that holds up in practice, based on how our own students structure their weeks.

| Week Part | Hours | What You Do |
|---|---|---|
| Mon, Wed, Fri evenings | 6 hours (2h x 3) | Live classes: Google Ads, Meta Ads, SEO, GA4, and the tools that make up the Full Stack program |
| Saturday | 3 to 4 hours | Hands-on practice: mock campaigns, keyword research, building out your portfolio |
| Sunday | 2 to 3 hours | Catching up on any missed class recording, reviewing notes, applying a concept at work |
| Total | 10 to 12 hours/week | Enough to stay on pace without touching your job hours |

Notice there's no "quit your job and study 6 hours a day" column in that table. That's deliberate. Nobody with rent, a family, or a salary they depend on should have to choose between eating and learning a new skill.

## How Recordings Cover the Classes You Miss

Work doesn't care about your class schedule. A client call runs long, your manager schedules a review at 7:30 PM, or you're just too drained on a Friday to sit through two hours of live teaching. That's exactly why every session comes with a recording and 12 months of replay access. You're not locked out if you miss a class, and you're not stuck re-learning from scratch six months later either, because the replay window stays open long after the live batch ends. I've had students who missed three straight weeks during a busy quarter at work and still finished the program on schedule, just by catching up on weekends using the recordings.

## Turn Your Current Job Into Your Practice Ground

This is the part working students underuse the most. Every company, no matter how small or how unrelated to marketing, has a gap somewhere. A dead Instagram page. A Google Business Profile nobody's touched in a year. A WhatsApp broadcast list that's never been segmented. Volunteer for it. Offer to run a small ₹2,000 test ad, fix the business listing, or take over the social calendar for a month.

You get two things out of this that a classroom alone can't give you. First, real data from a real audience, not a simulated exercise. Second, a portfolio line that says "grew engagement by X%" instead of "completed a course," which is what actually gets you shortlisted when you start interviewing. ## Mapping the 4-Month Timeline Against Your Job

Our [Full Stack Digital Marketing program](/programs/full-stack-digital-marketing) runs 4 months, and here's roughly how it maps onto a working schedule.

**Month 1:** Foundations. Google Ads, Meta Ads, SEO basics, GA4. You're learning tools, not yet running full campaigns at work.

**Month 2:** You start applying small pieces at your job. A better subject line for a work email, a rewritten Google Business Profile description, your first small ad test if your employer allows it.

**Month 3:** Bigger asks. This is when you volunteer to actually own a channel, even part-time, at your current workplace or for a friend's small business.

**Month 4:** Portfolio assembly and interview prep, while you're still employed and still earning.

## When to Tell Your Employer, and When Not To

Don't announce you're learning digital marketing to leave the moment you enroll. It creates unnecessary tension and sometimes gets you sidelined from projects before you've built anything worth showing. Instead, frame it as upskilling that benefits your current role, which is also usually true. "I'm learning Meta Ads, can I help run a small test for our page" is a very different conversation than "I'm training to quit."

The one exception: if your role already touches marketing even loosely, tell your manager early. You want access to real campaigns and real data, and that access usually needs sign-off.

## The Transition Moment

Switch jobs after the course and after you've built a portfolio, not before. That order matters more than almost anything else I tell working students. Quitting first and then studying under financial pressure is how people rush, cut corners, and end up with a certificate but no proof of actual work. Finishing first, portfolio in hand, means you walk into interviews as someone who already has results to show, not someone hoping to be trained on the job.

## I Won't Pretend This Is Easy

Four months of evening classes on top of a full workday is tiring. There will be Wednesdays where you'd rather go home and sleep than log into a 7 PM class. That's the honest trade-off, and I'd rather tell you that now than have you discover it in week three and feel blindsided. What makes it manageable is that it's temporary. Four months of tired evenings against years of a career you actually chose is a trade most of our 500 plus placed students, many of whom switched straight out of full-time jobs, tell me they'd make again.
