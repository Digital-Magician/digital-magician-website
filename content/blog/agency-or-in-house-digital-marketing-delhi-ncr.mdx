In-house pays a little more at the start. An agency pays considerably more by year three, because of what it teaches you in between.

If you want the short answer for a fresher in Delhi NCR: take the agency job for two years, then move in-house at a jump. That is the path most well-paid marketers I know in this region actually walked, including the ones who say they hated the first eighteen months.

## What each one pays, honestly

These are the ranges we see in offers to our graduates and in the wider Delhi NCR market. Gurugram sits at the top of each band, Noida in the middle, Delhi proper and Sonipat at the lower end.

| Stage | Agency | In-house |
|---|---|---|
| Fresher | ₹18,000 to ₹26,000 | ₹20,000 to ₹30,000 |
| 1 to 2 years | ₹28,000 to ₹40,000 | ₹30,000 to ₹42,000 |
| 3 to 4 years | ₹50,000 to ₹75,000 | ₹45,000 to ₹65,000 |
| 5 years plus | ₹80,000 to ₹1,50,000 | ₹70,000 to ₹1,20,000 |
| Freelance capacity alongside | High, after year two | Low, often contractually barred |

Two things in that table matter more than the numbers themselves.

The first is the crossover at year three. Agency people pass in-house people not because agencies are generous, but because three years of agency work gives you twelve accounts to talk about in an interview against one.

The second is the last row. Agency experience converts into freelance income far more easily, and for many people in Haryana that second income is what changes their finances rather than the salary line. We covered that route in [an agency job or freelancing](/blog/agency-job-or-freelance-sonipat-graduates).

## What an agency actually does to you in year one

It is not gentle, and you should know what you are buying.

You will be given between four and twelve accounts, which is more than anyone can do well. You will learn to triage. A client will call on Monday morning about a campaign you have not looked at since Thursday, and you will learn to check things before people ask. Something will break in a festival campaign at 9 PM and you will fix it.

That is the education. You cannot buy it, and in-house work rarely supplies it, because one business produces one kind of problem.

The cost is real. Expect 10 to 11 hour days in busy months and some weekend work. Expect to be underpaid relative to your output in year one, because your output includes a lot of mistakes someone senior has to catch.

## What in-house gives you that an agency cannot

Depth, and the thing most marketers never get: consequences.

In an agency you optimise a campaign and hand over a report. In-house, you see what happened in the warehouse when your campaign worked, which enquiries the sales team could not convert, what the product actually costs to deliver, and why the discount you wanted was refused.

That is business understanding, and it is what separates a ₹50,000 marketer from a ₹1,20,000 one later. It is simply slower to acquire at the start, and it is worth more once you already know the craft.

In-house is also the right first job for some people regardless of the above. If you are studying alongside, supporting a family, or commuting two hours, steadier hours are not a luxury. A marketer who lasts three years at a steady pace beats one who burns out in fourteen months.

## The question to ask in the interview

For an agency role: how many accounts will I handle, and who reviews my work before it goes live. Four to eight accounts with a senior reviewing is a good job. Fifteen accounts with nobody reviewing is a billing machine, and you will learn bad habits fast.

For an in-house role: who currently does this work, and what happens to them. If the answer is "an agency, and we are bringing it in", you are about to be the entire marketing department as a fresher. That can be a wonderful opportunity or an impossible job, and the difference is whether anyone senior will back your decisions.

Both answers tell you more than the salary number does.

## A day in each, since the job titles sound identical

The adverts for both roles use the same words. The days are not remotely alike, and knowing which one you would rather live is more useful than comparing salary bands.

**An agency day.** You arrive to eleven notifications. Two clients have replied to Friday's report, one is unhappy. A campaign for a third has spent its daily budget by 11 AM because someone widened the targeting. You spend the morning on whichever of those is loudest, the afternoon building creative for a launch on Thursday, and twenty minutes at 6 PM on the account you actually meant to work on today. You learn three things, two of them from mistakes. You go home tired and slightly behind.

**An in-house day.** You arrive to one business. The morning is a meeting about the new product launch where you mostly listen, because the decisions are about pricing and stock. The afternoon is your own campaign work, uninterrupted. At 4 PM the sales head tells you the leads from last week were poor quality, and the two of you work out that your form is attracting price shoppers. You fix one thing properly instead of eight things partly. You leave at 6:30.

Neither is better. But people who want variety wither in the second and people who want depth drown in the first, and you usually know which you are after a fortnight of either.

## The year two trap in both roles

Both paths have a point around eighteen months where careers stall, and the cause is different in each.

In an agency, the trap is becoming the person who executes well and never speaks to a client. Execution is replaceable and gets priced accordingly. The people who move from ₹30,000 to ₹60,000 are the ones who started presenting their own numbers to clients, which means volunteering for the uncomfortable call rather than letting a senior take it.

In-house, the trap is being the only marketer. Nobody reviews your work, so your bad habits never get corrected, and after two years you have two years of practising the same approach. The fix is deliberate: join a community, pay for one review of your accounts from someone senior, or move after year two.

Both traps are avoidable and both are extremely common, so it is worth knowing the shape of them before you walk into either.

## Freelancing alongside, and the rules nobody reads

For a lot of our graduates in Haryana, the second income matters more than the first salary, and the two paths treat it very differently.

Agency contracts frequently contain a clause barring outside client work, and some are enforced. In-house contracts usually do not, but employers may object if your client is anywhere near their industry. Read the contract before you sign, and if you intend to freelance, ask the question in the interview rather than finding out afterwards.

In practice, agency experience sells better to freelance clients because you can say you have handled accounts across industries, and that is exactly what a small business wants to hear. In-house experience sells well to one kind of client: another business in the same industry, which can be lucrative and narrow. ## Frequently asked questions

**Which pays more at the start?**
In-house, by roughly ₹2,000 to ₹5,000 a month. Agencies overtake around year three.

**Which teaches faster?**
An agency, because you handle many accounts and many failures in a short time.

**Are agency hours really that long?**
In busy months, yes. 10 to 11 hour days and some weekend work around launches.

**Can I move from in-house to an agency?**
Possible but harder. Agencies hire on breadth, so one employer reads as narrow.

**What should I ask in an agency interview?**
How many accounts, and who reviews your work before it goes live.
